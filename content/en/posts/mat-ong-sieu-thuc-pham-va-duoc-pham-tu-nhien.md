Honey's benefits are far greater than a simple life function and the short lifespan of this hardworking creature. It's easy to understand that bees know the uses and benefits of honey well. However, that's not all — this tiny creature has remarkably sophisticated ways of consuming honey, leaving humans endlessly puzzled as to how they can pick out the best honey to treat a sick bee among dozens of different types. Going back in history, a few centuries ago, honey wasn't even mentioned in the long list of health-beneficial functional foods for humans. Entomologist May Berenbaum, of the University of Illinois at Urbana-Champaign, says that even longtime beekeepers or bee scientists still didn't see honey as much different from sugar water. Humans need to learn a great deal from this tiny creature if we want to fully understand the nutritional nuances of honey. From then on, a wave of research emerged showing that honey contains many plant-derived chemicals — substances that affect the health of honey bees. The components in honey have a major impact on the health of this creature. Specifically, they help extend lifespan, increase tolerance to harsh natural conditions such as extreme cold and scorching heat, and boost resistance to infectious disease and wound healing. These discoveries have opened up a new path to help bees, which have been heavily affected by parasites, pesticides, and shrinking habitats in recent years.

**When did bees start making honey?**

Honey is a sweet, delicious food enjoyed with toast or hot tea, but thinking its benefits stop there would be a real mistake. That thick, viscous liquid you see is mainly sugar that bees use to sustain themselves. But this honey sugar contains many enzymes, vitamins, minerals, and organic molecules. These nutrients are what create the unique character of each type of honey, along with different health benefits for bees. Some other insects, like wasps and hornets, can also produce honey, but not every type of bee can produce it in quantities sufficient for storage. Honey bees, in order to gain this ability, had to go through millions of years of evolution. Honey bees split off from the wasp family around 120 million years ago, at the peak of the evolution and proliferation of flowering plants. Along with the rich diversification of flowers, and a certain shift in bee behavior toward feeding larvae with pollen instead of insects, this drove the evolution of nearly 20,000 bee species. To become experts in honey production, beyond their inherent diligence and hard work, bees needed a few extra tricks. Bees began adding a bit of nectar and pollen, kneading it into specially shaped pellets that were more convenient to carry. They also developed wax-secreting glands to keep the liquid nectar and solid pollen separated in distinct areas.

**![Honey: a natural superfood and medicine](/images/posts/mat-ong-sieu-thuc-pham-va-duoc-pham-tu-nhien/sieu-thuc-pham-mat-ong-1.jpg)A flexible building material**

According to Christina Grozinger, "Wax allows for the creation of a very flexible building material." She researches the mechanisms behind bees' social behavior and health. Have you ever wondered why beehives are hexagonal in shape? It turns out the hexagon is the most efficient shape for storing nectar, since the cells interlock extremely tightly. According to Grozinger, "this is a feat of engineering." Building small, uniform cells has another advantage: because the surface area is larger, water evaporates faster, and lower humidity means bacterial growth is more strongly inhibited. The process of making the honey that fills those cells begins when a bee draws up nectar. It might look like the bee is eating the nectar, but instead, it's transported somewhere other than its stomach — to another location where it mixes with a variety of enzymes.

Building small, uniform cells has another advantage: a larger surface area means water evaporates faster, and less water means less bacterial growth.

Among these, one notable enzyme is invertase, which splits the sucrose molecule in nectar into glucose and fructose — even though bees were found to lack the gene to produce this sucrose-cleaving enzyme themselves, and instead rely on another type of bacteria living in their gut. Once back at the hive, the bee carrying the nectar regurgitates it for other bees. This mouth-to-mouth journey then reduces water content and adds more enzymes, continuing the process of breaking down the nectar and inhibiting bacteria. The bees then place this mixture into a cell inside the hexagonal hive, continuously fanning their wings to help more water evaporate. The enzyme glucose oxidase then acts to convert some of the glucose into gluconic acid, which helps preserve the honey better. The chemical reactions that occur here help lower the pH, increase acidity, and generate additional hydrogen peroxide, which helps prevent bacterial growth at low concentrations. Other enzymes are added to keep this hydrogen peroxide level under control. Nurse bees then feed the processed honey to other bees and keep a portion in reserve for cold, stormy days.

**![Honey: a natural superfood and medicine](/images/posts/mat-ong-sieu-thuc-pham-va-duoc-pham-tu-nhien/sieu-thuc-pham-mat-ong-2.jpg)Sweet medicine**

Berenbaum's connection to honey lies in nectar itself. She knew that nectar contains a blend of many plant chemicals — compounds that ward off pests and stimulate plant growth and metabolism. She sensed that these plant chemicals were there to be carried along when bees convert nectar into honey. She wanted to know what effect they had on bees. Berenbaum began investigating the various chemicals found in honey. In 1998, her research team discovered that different types of honey have different antioxidant capacities, depending on the type of flower the bees collected from. This is what sparked her interest. Not long after, she found that honey bees fed sugar water mixed with two plant chemicals found in honey — p-coumaric acid and a powerful antioxidant called quercetin — withstood pesticides better and lived longer than those fed only sugar water. She and her colleagues reported this finding in the journal Insects in 2017. Abscisic acid strengthens bees' immune responses, improving wound-healing time and cold tolerance. What's more, other plant chemicals help reduce the impact of parasites — a major cause of honey bee decline. For example, using a syrup containing thymol, found in thyme, helped cut the number of fungal spores in an infected bee by half.

Humans can have a positive impact on bees by keeping a variety of different honey types in the hive. Plant chemicals have also proven capable of inhibiting the bacteria that cause larval rot disease. This practice is recommended in the US, where this disease spreads aggressively and can wipe out entire hives. Certain other plant chemicals also enhance the activity of genes responsible for detoxification and immunity in the body.

For example, when bees are exposed to a nectar chemical like anabasine, a gene responsible for producing an antibacterial protein gets stimulated into production, according to a research team writing in the Journal of Economic Entomology in 2017.

Plant chemicals can protect bee health by preserving the living environment for microorganisms inside and on the bee's body — the bee microbiome. According to research published last year in the journal Applied and Environmental Microbiology, chemicals such as caffeine, gallic acid, p-coumaric acid, and kaempferol can all help increase the diversity of gut bacteria in honey bees.

When honey bees have a healthy gut microbiome, they experience fewer parasitic infections. Honey bees can even choose the type of honey that improves their health whenever they fall ill. Entomologist Silvio Erler and his team ran an experiment offering 4 types of honey to bees infected with parasites. The bees tended to choose sunflower honey, as it turned out to be the best remedy for this illness, with the highest level of antibacterial activity.

## ![Honey: a natural superfood and medicine](/images/posts/mat-ong-sieu-thuc-pham-va-duoc-pham-tu-nhien/sieu-thuc-pham-mat-ong-3.jpg)Do honey bees heal themselves?

Although honey is an effective remedy that boosts immunity along with many other health benefits, it's not a miracle cure for every ailment. According to statistics, beekeepers in the US had to sacrifice 45% of their bee colonies within a single year, from April 2020 to April 2021 — the second-worst year since the nonprofit organization Bee Informed Partnership began conducting surveys and research in 2006.

Even though beekeepers leave some honey in the hive, having multiple types of honey in a hive brings certain benefits. Research shows that different types of honey, gathered by different bees from sainfoin flowers, sunflowers, and many other species, help ward off different types of bacteria.

Erler compares this diversity to a pharmacy, where each symptom has a different specific medicine. However, this pharmacy can only sell the medicine if that particular flower is available. It's not just a matter of quantity and diversity, but also consistency throughout the growing season, according to Berenbaum, co-author of a review on the impact of honey on bee health published in the Annual Review of Entomology 2021. Vast monoculture fields such as almonds, apples, pumpkins, and pears, where bees are brought in for pollination, are struggling with a lack of biodiversity. Improving floral diversity helps bees stay healthier, according to Arathi Seshadri, an entomologist at the USDA's Honey Bee Health Lab in Davis, California. The USDA encourages landowners to convert part of their farmland into wild areas so pollinators can be sustained. Even better nutrition alone won't solve all the problems bees face. But ensuring honey bees have their own "medicine cabinet" is helpful, Erler says. He also suggests that beekeepers could leave a portion of honey gathered from a variety of flowers in the hive to help ensure their colony's health. Berenbaum began her research because she felt honey deserved much more attention than it was getting.
